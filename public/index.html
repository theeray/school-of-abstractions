Steve Sundahl · the TAD logo

A shared frame.
Individual voices.

A designer’s account connects the TAD emblem to Vienna’s square monograms and the Bauhaus use of primary colors. In this painting, those influences enter a further conversation with De Stijl.

The designer’s words · supplied by Eric R. Carlson

“I will just say that part of the inspiration was Bauhaus in the use of primary colors. The primary inspiration was the Austrian secession group a decade or so earlier. I admire the mixture of organic line with geometry in their work. In their publication Ver Sacrum all the artists signed their contributions with a monogram of their initials inside a square. I thought it was a great metaphor for unity and individualism.“

Steve Sundahl · designer of the TAD logo · verbatim statement

“Secession” is a name and an action.

The Vienna Secession, or Wiener Secession, began in 1897 when artists around Gustav Klimt broke away from Vienna’s conservative Künstlerhaus association. An artists’ association with varied approaches, it brought painting, architecture, and design into conversation. Ver Sacrum—“sacred spring”—was its journal, published from 1898 to 1903.

What connects—and what remains distinct

Vienna → Bauhaus

Secessionist designers including Koloman Moser and Josef Hoffmann reformed applied-art teaching and worked across design and craft. Letterform Archive describes that Viennese pedagogy as preparing the ground for schools such as Bauhaus. This is a connection through methods and education, rather than a claim that Vienna invented primary colors.

Letterform Archive’s research ↗

De Stijl ↔ Bauhaus

De Stijl’s reduced vocabulary of primary colors and geometry has its own history: MoMA identifies Bart van der Leck’s palette as an influence on Mondrian. Theo taught De Stijl courses to Bauhaus students in 1922. A direct exchange is documented here; a simple Vienna–Bauhaus–De Stijl sequence would obscure the chronology.

The logo inside this painting

Steve explicitly names Vienna Secession and Bauhaus. The painting adds De Stijl affinities through Mondrian’s grid and Theo’s glass: T belongs to the architecture, A transmits light, and D becomes a form Euclid measures. One emblem takes three material lives while retaining its shared identity.

Quotation, chronology, and image notes

Steve’s statement is reproduced exactly as supplied by Eric. The exhibition distinguishes the designer’s recollection from verified dates and from its own interpretations. The monogram sheet is from the 1902 catalogue; it is not labelled as a page from Ver Sacrum. None of these images is an AI reconstruction.

Red, yellow, and blue were used systematically long before these movements—for example, in Jacob Christoph Le Blon’s eighteenth-century color printing. Vienna Secession did not originate the primary-color system. Smithsonian Libraries’ history of color ↗
